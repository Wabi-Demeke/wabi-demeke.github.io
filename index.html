Who I Am

About Me

I am a Ph.D. graduate in Mechanical Engineering from KAIST, currently a Postdoctoral Research Associate in the Department of Chemistry at the University of Wyoming.

My work sits at the intersection of computational physics and AI/ML. I have a strong foundation in scientific workflows that pair physics-based data generation through multiphysics FEM simulation with ML model training and careful validation. My recent work includes physics-informed ML, ML-based design optimization, and multi-agent LLM systems that help automate atomistic modeling pipelines.

I am looking for R&D Scientist and Research Engineer roles in Applied AI and Machine Learning, where my experience in physics-informed ML, surrogate model optimization, and simulation pipelines can be put to good use.

What I Build

Research Focus

Structured around four core questions that define the frontiers of computational science and autonomous discovery.

Q1

Physics-Informed Machine Learning (PIML)

Embedding physical laws into AI models so they remain reliable when simulation or experimental data is scarce. Our approach in npj Computational Materials (2025) generalized to unseen materials without retraining, and the work was featured on Phys.org.

PINNsNeural OperatorsZero-Shot Generalization
Q2

Optimization In Vast Design Space

AI-driven optimization frameworks to search astronomical design landscapes. Screened 1.4 × 10¹⁴ material combinations for Segmented Thermoelectric Generators, achieving 1.91× power density and 1.5× conversion efficiency over benchmarks. Published in Applied Energy (2024).

Deep Neural NetworksGenetic AlgorithmsActive Learning
Q3

Data-Efficient Optimization

Optimizing complex engineering problems where data is expensive or scarce. Improved wave localization intensity by 36% and power generation by 1.5× using only 5.9% of the data required by typical DNN methods. Published in IJSS (2026).

Bayesian OptimizationMetamaterialsPhononic Crystals
Schematic coming soon
Q4

Multi-Agent Systems

Building autonomous multi-agent LLM frameworks that plan, execute, and self-correct atomistic transport simulations (DFTB) on HPC clusters — reducing expert setup time from days to minutes. Coupled with crystal graph convolutional neural networks for rapid prediction of electronic transport in metal-organic frameworks (MOFs). Current focus at University of Wyoming.
